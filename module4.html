Module 4

Intelligent Agents

Module Overview

The Intelligent Agents module explores how software agents are designed, how they reason and plan, how they communicate with each other, and how Large Language Models (LLMs) can support planning and task decomposition. The module combines a team design proposal for an LLM-powered planning agent, an individual implementation and presentation, and this reflective e-Portfolio.

This module is in progress. Units, artefacts and reflections are added as I work through the module.

Reflection

The most useful idea for me in this unit was the difference between a normal program and an agent. A script runs the same steps every time, while an agent notices what is happening around it and decides what to do. As a network engineer, I could see this clearly: our monitoring tools raise alerts, but they do not decide anything. This made me think more carefully about how much freedom I would actually give an agent, which became the main theme of my first discussion post.

Key Topics

  • Why agents need a precise language: natural language is ambiguous, while first-order logic (FOL) gives each sentence one meaning that is either true or false.
  • Constants, variables, predicates and functions; connectives (∧, ∨, ¬, ⇒, ⇔).
  • Quantifiers (∀ and ∃) and why their order changes the meaning.
  • Knowledge bases (TELL, ASK) and knowledge engineering (Russell and Norvig, 2021, Chapter 8).

First-order logic was not easy for me at first, mainly because of the number of new symbols. What helped was linking each idea to something I already know from networking, such as treating a predicate like a yes/no check on a device. I also realised that the shared data format in our team project is a simple version of the same idea: all agents need to agree on the meaning of the information they exchange.

This unit connected directly to our team project. Before it, I was thinking about the system only as a set of steps. After studying Bratman's idea of commitment, I understood why our Planner should stick to the plan the user approves and only change it when the results are weak. Unit 4Hybrid Agent ArchitecturesKey TopicsLearning ActivitiesReflection

Key Topics

  • Why hybrid architectures: combining fast reactive behaviour with planning by dividing an agent into layers (Wooldridge, 2009).
  • Horizontal layering, where every layer proposes an action and a control subsystem chooses (e.g. TouringMachines).
  • Vertical layering, one-pass and two-pass, with bottom-up activation and top-down execution (e.g. InteRRaP).
  • The 3T architecture: planning, sequencing and skills (Bonasso et al., 1997).
  • Critique of layered designs: practical and widely used, but harder to analyse, and layer interactions can be complex.

Learning Activities

  • Studied Section 5.2 of Wooldridge (2009) and the Unit 4 lecturecast.
  • Attended the Unit 4 seminar on hybrid architectures, their trade-offs (inter-layer latency, state consistency and design complexity) and the team assessment.
  • Used the unit to finalise our team's architecture: a Planner-led multi-agent system inspired by the functional separation in 3T.

Reflection

This unit helped me describe our team design honestly. At first I wanted to call it a vertical layered architecture, but I realised that 3T, InteRRaP and TouringMachines all describe layers inside one agent, while our system has four separate agents. So we described it as "inspired by" 3T rather than claiming it is 3T. The seminar also made me think about the weakness of our design: the Planner coordinates everything, so it can become a single point of failure. This is why limited retries and clear error messages became part of our design.

Unit 5Agent CommunicationKey TopicsLearning ActivitiesReflection

Key Topics

  • Speech act theory: speaking is a form of action, and every message has a purpose (performative) and a content (Searle, 1969).
  • KQML as the outer language of a message (performative, sender, receiver, reply links) and KIF as the content language, based on first-order logic (Finin et al., 1994).
  • Ontologies as a shared vocabulary, and aligning different ontologies through dialogue without revealing everything (Payne and Tamma, 2014).
  • Weaknesses of KQML: loose semantics, poor interoperability between implementations and no commissive performatives.

Learning Activities

  • Watched the Unit 5 lecturecast and read Finin et al. (1994) and Payne and Tamma (2014).
  • Posted my Initial Post for Collaborative Discussion 2 on agent communication languages (see below).
  • Continued leading the team project: reviewed the first drafts of all four sections and gave feedback.

Reflection

KQML was easier for me to understand when I compared it to a network packet: the KQML part works like the header, saying who sends the message, to whom and what kind of message it is, while the KIF content is like the payload. This unit also explained a decision in our team project. We chose a small set of message types inspired by KQML instead of the full language, because all our agents are built by one team and in one language. We kept the clear intent of each message without the extra complexity.

e-Portfolio Activity: Creating Agent Dialogues

Task: create a KQML and KIF dialogue between Alice, an agent that procures stock, and Bob, an agent that controls the stock levels of a warehouse. Alice asks Bob about the available stock of 50-inch televisions and the number of HDMI slots they have.

; 1. Alice asks Bob about the stock of 50-inch TVs
(ask-one
   :sender      Alice
   :receiver    Bob
   :language    KIF
   :ontology    warehouse-stock
   :reply-with  q1
   :content     (stock-level tv-50inch ?quantity))

; 2. Bob replies: 80 units in stock
(tell
   :sender       Bob
   :receiver     Alice
   :language     KIF
   :ontology     warehouse-stock
   :in-reply-to  q1
   :content      (stock-level tv-50inch 80))

; 3. Alice asks Bob about the number of HDMI slots
(ask-one
   :sender      Alice
   :receiver    Bob
   :language    KIF
   :ontology    warehouse-stock
   :reply-with  q2
   :content     (hdmi-slots tv-50inch ?slots))

; 4. Bob replies: 3 HDMI slots
(tell
   :sender       Bob
   :receiver     Alice
   :language     KIF
   :ontology     warehouse-stock
   :in-reply-to  q2
   :content      (hdmi-slots tv-50inch 3))

Explanation. In this dialogue, Alice uses the ask-one performative because she needs one specific value from Bob, and Bob uses tell to give her the answer. Each question has its own label (:reply-with q1 and q2), and Bob uses :in-reply-to in his answers, so Alice knows which answer belongs to which question. This is useful when an agent sends more than one question at the same time.

The content is written in KIF, where the relation comes first, then the object, then the value. A variable such as ?quantity shows the value Alice is asking for. Both agents use the same ontology (warehouse-stock), so they understand terms such as stock-level and hdmi-slots in the same way. Without this shared ontology, Bob might not understand what Alice is asking for (Finin et al., 1994; Wooldridge, 2009).

Reflection

Writing the dialogue myself showed me small details that I missed when only reading about KQML. My first attempts used the wrong performative for Bob's answer and the same label for both questions, which would have made it impossible for Alice to match each answer to its question. This made the role of :reply-with and :in-reply-to very clear to me. It also connected to our team project, where each message carries a question identifier for the same reason, and where the Planner routes all messages in a way similar to a facilitator.

Initial Post (25 October 2026)

When I look back at how my work as a network engineer has changed, the reasons behind the rise of agent-based systems feel very familiar. Wooldridge (2009) links it to five trends in computing: ubiquity, interconnection, intelligence, delegation and human-orientation. I can see most of them in my own workplace. Our network is no longer in one building or even one country; we now support branches on several continents. On top of that, attacks and updates arrive so quickly that, honestly, a human team cannot keep up with all of them. At that point, handing some of the work to software that can act on its own becomes a practical need rather than a choice (Wooldridge and Jennings, 1995).

We have already seen the benefits. We use an automated monitoring system that checks the performance of all our devices every day, from IP phones to switches and firewalls. It never stops, while our team works seven-hour shifts, and it catches details that we sometimes missed during manual checks. For me, this is autonomy and reactivity in practice.

That said, we have been careful. We do not let these systems make decisions on critical parts of the network. If an automated tool decided to block traffic that turned out to be important, it could take a live channel off air, and in broadcasting that is a serious risk. Wooldridge (2009) describes this balance as adjustable autonomy: the decision should go back to a person when the potential harm is high. In my view, two things would make us more comfortable: letting the agent suggest an action that an engineer approves first (Parasuraman, Sheridan and Wickens, 2000), and testing it for a long period in a lab environment before it goes live.

So, I see agents as a real benefit for organisations, but trust has to be built step by step, especially where the cost of a mistake is high.

Feedback Received and My Replies

  • Agnieszka (26 Oct): argued that waiting for human approval can be too slow during time-critical incidents. My reply: autonomy should depend on risk, with small urgent actions automated and high-impact actions still approved by an engineer.
  • Mark (27 Oct): pointed out that people may over-rely on the agent, and that approval does not fix a poorly chosen objective. My reply: clarified that our current system is monitoring only, and agreed that agents should explain their reasoning and that thresholds need regular review.
  • Adam (31 Oct): highlighted skill loss through automation (Bainbridge, 1983) and suggested shadow mode and rollback. My reply: shadow mode gives a measurable way to judge readiness, and keeping people's skills sharp should be part of the design.

My Peer Responses

  • To Agnieszka: questioned whether wider adoption of agents means deeper trust, and supported hybrid designs where the LLM plans and rules constrain actions.
  • To Mark: used a networking example of a tool rewarded for closing alerts quickly while faults remain, and suggested involving affected stakeholders when defining objectives.
  • To Adam: noted a tension between upgrading agents individually and unpredictable interactions between agents, and answered his question using reliability and the cost of errors (Parasuraman, Sheridan and Wickens, 2000) and adjustable autonomy in teams (Scerri, Pynadath and Tambe, 2002).

Summary Post

In my initial post, I said that agent-based systems became popular because of the trends Wooldridge (2009) describes, especially interconnection and delegation. I also said that important decisions should stay with people. The agent suggests, an engineer approves (Parasuraman, Sheridan and Wickens, 2000), and trust is built step by step.

My colleagues made me look at this again from three angles. Agnieszka pointed out that waiting for approval can be too slow when an attack is moving fast. That made me realise one rule for everything does not work. Small, urgent actions can be automated, while anything that could cause serious harm should still need approval. For me, that is what adjustable autonomy really means (Wooldridge, 2009).

Mark showed me that approval on its own is not enough. People can start trusting the agent’s suggestions too easily (Onnasch et al., 2014), and approving a decision does not fix a wrong objective. Adam then added something I had not thought about at all: if automation does the routine work, people may lose the skills they need when they have to step in (Bainbridge, 1983). His ideas of shadow mode and rollback gave me a practical way to measure trust instead of just feeling it.

Unit 3 helped me put this together. A reactive agent is fast but does not plan, and a deliberative agent thinks carefully but can be too slow (Brooks, 1991; Wooldridge, 2009). Honestly, after this discussion, I think organisations need something in between: quick reactions for low-risk events, and a slower, more careful layer when a mistake would be costly.

So my view has changed. I still believe agents bring real benefits, but what matters most is not how much freedom they have. It is how well that freedom is designed.

How My View Changed

I started the discussion believing that critical decisions should always wait for human approval. My colleagues showed me that this can be too slow, that approval alone does not guarantee good decisions, and that automation can weaken the skills people need when they step in. I now think the real question is not how much autonomy an agent has, but how well that autonomy is designed.

Question: What are the potential advantages and disadvantages of the use of agent communication languages such as KQML? How do they compare with method invocation in Python or Java?

Initial Post

KQML, which stands for Knowledge Query and Manipulation Language, provides a common language that allows agents to communicate and exchange information and requests. It helps each agent understand the purpose of the message it receives (Finin et al., 1994).

A main advantage of KQML is that it makes communication easier between agents built with different technologies (Labrou, Finin and Peng, 1999). It also allows agents to request information or send results without knowing how the other agent works. Another advantage is that new agents can be added without redesigning the whole system, as long as they follow the same communication protocol.

On the other hand, KQML may increase system complexity because of the different types of messages. It also does not guarantee that all agents understand the message content in the same way, so a shared ontology is needed (Wooldridge, 2009). Exchanging and processing messages also adds time and computational cost.

The main difference is that KQML sends a request to an independent agent, which can accept or reject it based on its capabilities and goals. In contrast, method invocation directly calls a method to perform a specific task, without giving the receiving component the same level of decision-making autonomy (Jennings, 2000).

In my opinion, KQML is more suitable for systems where independent agents need to communicate and cooperate, while method invocation is more suitable for simple systems with predefined tasks. For example, a multi-agent system can use a simple communication approach inspired by KQML, with only a few message types such as request, result, error and done. This keeps the intent of each message clear without adding complexity. Using a multi-agent system does not necessarily mean using the full KQML language, but rather choosing what suits the project's requirements and size.

Peer Responses

To Igor:

Thank you, Igor, for a very detailed post. I especially liked your point that the real difference between KQML and method invocation is about who decides, not how the message is delivered.

I agree with your conclusion that method invocation belongs inside an agent and an agent protocol between agents. In practice, this seems like a balanced approach: inside each agent, normal method calls keep the code simple and easy to test, while between agents a small set of clear message types can show the intent of each request without the full complexity of KQML (Finin et al., 1994).

I also found your point about A2A and LLMs interesting, especially the risk that a misunderstanding appears as a plausible wrong answer rather than an error. In my view, this makes human oversight more important when an LLM infers intent. For example, in systems where an LLM acts as a controller that plans subtasks and assigns them to other components, as in HuggingGPT (Shen et al., 2023), asking the user to confirm the plan before it runs could catch a misunderstood request early, before it affects the final result.

Do you think newer protocols such as A2A should include a simple confirmation step like this, or should it stay outside the protocol, in the application itself?

To Mark:

Thank you, Mark, for your clear post. I liked your idea of "agreement without disclosure", because this is something method invocation cannot do.

I agree that method invocation is the better choice when the components are known and trusted. I think the choice depends on the relationship between the components, not only on the technology. If one team designs all the agents, a small set of simple message types may be enough, because all the agents already understand the same meanings. But if the agents are built by different organisations, they need a more complete language and a shared ontology (Labrou, Finin and Peng, 1999).

I also agree with your point about the cost of dialogue. More messages mean more delay, and if the performatives are not clear, the agents can still misunderstand each other. This is one of the reasons FIPA ACL later gave messages a clearer meaning than KQML (Wooldridge, 2009).

However, I am not sure the privacy benefit is important in every system. When the agents belong to the same organisation, they usually do not need to hide information from each other, so the extra messages may add cost but give little benefit.

In your opinion, which kinds of systems really need this level of privacy between agents?
